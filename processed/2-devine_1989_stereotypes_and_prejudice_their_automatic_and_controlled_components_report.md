### Executive Summary

The paper investigates whether automatic stereotype activation and controlled inhibition differ between high- and low-prejudice individuals. It finds that both groups possess equivalent knowledge of Black stereotypes and exhibit equally strong automatic activation, but only low-prejudice participants can intentionally inhibit stereotype‑consistent thoughts, highlighting a controlled process distinction. This supports a model separating automatic activation from controlled belief regulation in prejudice.

### Background and Motivation

The paper addresses the unresolved question of whether high- and low-prejudice individuals differ in their automatic and controlled responses to stereotyped group members, a gap highlighted despite extensive evidence that stereotypes are well‑learned, automatically activated associations (e.g., Dovidio, Evans, & Tyler 1986; Smith & Branscombe 1985) and that controlled processes can override such activation (Higgins & King 1981). Prior work has shown early formation of stereotypes (Allport 1954) and the distinction between cultural stereotypes and personal beliefs (Katz 1976), yet it remains unclear if prejudice level moderates the interplay of automatic activation and controlled inhibition. This study therefore builds on these foundations to test knowledge equivalence, automatic activation equivalence, and controlled inhibition across prejudice levels.

### Findings Summary

Across the three experiments, high- and low-prejudice participants showed equivalent knowledge of the Black stereotype (Experiment 1) and produced comparable stereotype‑congruent evaluations of ambiguous behavior when conscious monitoring was blocked (Experiment 2), supporting the claim that automatic stereotype activation is equally strong and independent of prejudice level. In contrast, only low-prejudice participants demonstrated the ability to suppress automatically activated stereotype‑consistent thoughts and replace them with equality‑focused thoughts in a directed thought‑listing task (Experiment 3), indicating that controlled inhibition can override automatic activation for those motivated to maintain a nonprejudiced identity. Thus, while automatic activation appears uniform across prejudice groups, controlled processes diverge, allowing low-prejudice individuals to inhibit stereotypic responses.

## Abstract

*Preserved from source without summarization.*

Three studies tested basic assumptions derived from a theoretical model based on the dissociation of automatic and controlled processes involved in prejudice. Study 1 supported the model's assumption that high- and low-prejudice persons are equally knowledgeable of the cultural stereotype. The model suggests that the stereotype is automatically activated in the presence of a member (or some symbolic equivalent) of the stereotyped group and that low-prejudice responses require controlled inhibition of the automatically activated stereotype. Study 2, which examined the effects of automatic stereotype activation on the evaluation of ambiguous stereotype-relevant behaviors performed by a race-unspecified person, suggested that when subjects' ability to consciously monitor stereotype activation is precluded, both high- and low-prejudice subjects produce stereotype-congruent evaluations of ambiguous behaviors. Study 3 examined high- and low-prejudice subjects' responses in a consciously directed thought-listing task. Consistent with the model, only low-prejudice subjects inhibited the automatically activated stereotype-congruent thoughts and replaced them with thoughts reflecting equality and negations of the stereotype. The relation between stereotypes and prejudice and implications for prejudice reduction are discussed.
