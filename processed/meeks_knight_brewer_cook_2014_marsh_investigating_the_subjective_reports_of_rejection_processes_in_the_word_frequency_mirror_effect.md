## Paper Synthesis

*Paper: Meeks, Knight, Brewer, Cook 2014 - Marsh Investigating the subjective reports of rejection processes in the word frequency mirror effect*

### Executive Summary

The paper investigates whether the lower false‑alarm rate for low‑frequency words stems from a metacognitive memorability judgment or a lack of familiarity, using subjective rejection classifications across three experiments. It finds that low‑frequency items are more often rejected on the basis of perceived memorability in mixed‑list contexts, but this effect disappears in pure‑list designs, while the basic correct‑rejection advantage persists. These results suggest that memorability‑based rejection contributes to the word‑frequency mirror effect only when contrasting frequency items are present, highlighting the role of test context in metacognitive judgments.

### Reading Recommendation

**Read Selectively**

The paper offers novel insights into how word‑frequency context influences memorability‑based rejection judgments, but methodological gaps (no power analysis, incomplete ANOVA reporting, uncorrected multiple comparisons) and overextension to distinctiveness without measurement limit its rigor. Experts should focus on the experimental methods, key results (especially the interaction of list composition with memorability judgments), and the authors' theoretical discussion.

### Background and Motivation

The paper addresses the unresolved question of whether the lower false‑alarm advantage for low‑frequency words stems from a metacognitive memorability assessment or from a lack of pre‑experimental familiarity, a gap left open by earlier mirror‑effect research. This gap is motivated by classic findings of the word‑frequency mirror effect (Glanzer & Adams, 1985) and by evidence that low‑frequency items are more memorable (Benjamin, 2003) yet less familiar (Underwood & Freund, 1970). Recent work showing independent mirror effects of word frequency and context variability (Cook et al., 2006) further underscores the need to disentangle these competing processes.

### Theoretical Stance

**Overall stance: Consistent Theoretical**

The introduction foregrounds a memorability‑based rejection account and the discussion repeatedly returns to this account as the primary explanation, while treating alternative accounts as competing or supplementary explanations without abandoning the focal theory.

#### Theories Discussed

- **memorability‑based rejection process** *(focal)*: A metacognitive judgment that low‑frequency items are more memorable, leading participants to reject them as new because they would have been remembered if studied.
- **familiarity‑based rejection** *(alternative)*: Rejection of low‑frequency items due to their lower pre‑experimental familiarity, producing weaker implicit associative responses.
- **distinctiveness heuristic** *(alternative)*: Use of an item’s distinctiveness (e.g., diagnostic details) as evidence for non‑studied status; low‑frequency items are inherently more distinctive.
- **inherent/secondary distinctiveness account** *(background)*: The notion that the infrequent occurrence of low‑frequency words makes them intrinsically more distinctive and thus more memorable.
- **recall‑to‑reject mechanism** *(background)*: A process whereby participants retrieve contextual information from study to determine that a test item was not previously encountered.
- **functional account of recollection rejection** *(background)*: A view that recollection‑based rejection depends on contextual factors and criterial status of recollection judgments.

### Findings Summary

Across the three experiments, low‑frequency words (LWF) consistently yielded higher correct‑rejection rates than high‑frequency words (HWF), but the subjective basis for this advantage differed by design. In mixed‑list (within‑subjects) experiments 1 and 2, participants were more likely to attribute LWF rejections to a “Would Have Remembered” (memorability‑based) process, whereas HWF rejections were linked to “Lacks Familiarity”; the recall‑to‑reject option showed no frequency effect. Experiment 3, which used pure‑list (between‑subjects) frequency manipulation, replicated the LWF correct‑rejection advantage but eliminated the memorability‑based classification difference, with no significant differences in “Would Have Remembered” or “Lacks Familiarity” responses between LWF and HWF items. Context variability produced parallel, independent effects on hits, remembers, knows, and subjective classifications, confirming that WF and CV each drive mirror‑effect patterns. Thus, the memorability‑based rejection advantage for LWF items appears contingent on the presence of contrasting frequency items in the test context, while the basic correct‑rejection benefit persists across list compositions.

### Evidence–Claim Alignment

#### Supported Claims

- Low‑frequency (LWF) words produce higher correct‑rejection rates than high‑frequency (HWF) words – supported by Experiment 1 t(24)=4.959, p<.001 and replicated in Experiments 2 and 3 (significant main effects of WF on false‑alarm rates).
- In mixed‑list designs (Experiments 1 and 2), participants attribute LWF correct rejections more often to a “Would Have Remembered” (memorability‑based) response than HWF items – supported by Experiment 1 t(24)=4.381, p<.001 and Experiment 2 main effects of WF on Would‑Have‑Remembered responses (F(1,33)=15.808, p<.001).
- HWF correct rejections are more often classified as “Lacks Familiarity” – supported by Experiment 1 conditionalized Lacks‑Familiarity responses (t(24)=4.186, p<.001) and Experiment 2 main effect of CV on Lacks‑Familiarity (F(1,33)=9.059, p=.005).
- Word frequency (WF) and context variability (CV) each produce independent mirror‑effect patterns on hits, remembers, knows, and subjective classifications – supported by Experiment 2 main effects of WF and CV on hit rates, Remember, Know, and Would‑Have‑Remembered responses (all p≤.001).
- In pure‑list (between‑subjects) designs, the LWF correct‑rejection advantage persists even though memorability‑based classifications do not differ – supported by Experiment 3 main effect of WF on correct‑rejection rates (F(1,67)=4.066, p=.048) and null WF effects on Would‑Have‑Remembered and Lacks‑Familiarity responses (both p>.4).


#### Unsupported or Weakly Supported Claims

- The claim that memorability‑driven rejection fully accounts for the LWF correct‑rejection advantage – evidence is weak because alternative processes (Lacks‑Familiarity, Recall‑to‑Reject) showed non‑significant differences (p>.12) and were not integrated into the theoretical narrative.
- The assertion that distinctiveness (inherent or secondary) explains the effect – no direct measure of item distinctiveness was collected, so the claim extends beyond the presented data.
- The prediction that participants would show no difference in hit rates for LWF versus HWF items in pure‑list designs – Experiment 3 found no significant WF effect on hits (p=.283), but the authors still discuss a “failure to find some of these effects” without providing a definitive conclusion, leaving the claim ambiguous.


#### Contradictions or Tensions

- Experiments 1 and 2 show a strong memorability‑based classification advantage for LWF items, yet Experiment 3 (pure‑list) eliminates this classification difference while the correct‑rejection advantage remains, creating tension with the overarching claim that memorability drives the rejection benefit.
- The discussion generalizes the findings to a distinctiveness heuristic despite the absence of distinctiveness metrics, contradicting the earlier emphasis on memorability as the primary mechanism.
- Authors claim a “mirror effect” for both WF and CV, but the marginal CV effect on correct‑rejection rates in Experiment 2 (p=.058) is presented as supportive, which conflicts with the standard significance threshold and weakens the claim of independent CV mirror effects.


### Strengths

- Clear experimental progression across three studies that isolates word frequency, context variability, and list composition effects on recognition memory.
- Use of novel subjective classification categories (Would Have Remembered, Lacks Familiarity, Recall‑to‑Reject) to directly probe participants' metacognitive rejection processes.
- Consistent replication of the low‑frequency correct‑rejection advantage across mixed‑list and pure‑list designs, providing converging evidence for the mirror effect.
- Demonstration that word frequency and context variability each produce independent main effects on hits, Remember, Know, and subjective classifications, supporting the notion of multiple contributing mechanisms.
- Inclusion of conditionalized response analyses that control for overall response tendencies, strengthening the interpretation of frequency‑specific classification patterns.
- Transparent reporting of effect sizes (Cohen's d, η², g²) for significant findings, facilitating assessment of practical significance.


### Weaknesses and Concerns

- Theoretical overreach: the authors extend their findings to a distinctiveness account without measuring item distinctiveness, weakening the claim that distinctiveness explains the memorability advantage.
- Alternative processes (Lacks Familiarity, Recall‑to‑Reject) showed non‑significant effects yet are downplayed; the narrative that memorability fully accounts for the rejection advantage is not fully supported by the data.
- Statistical reporting gaps: the main effect of Response Type in the 2 × 3 ANOVA (Experiment 1) is omitted, and multiple pairwise t‑tests are presented without correction for family‑wise error, raising concerns about Type I inflation.
- Methodological omissions: no a priori power analyses, absent counterbalancing descriptions, and insufficient exclusion criteria across all experiments limit confidence in the robustness of the results.
- Reliability of the subjective classification measures is not assessed, leaving unclear whether the reported response categories consistently capture the intended constructs.
- Marginal findings (e.g., context variability effect on correct rejections in Experiment 2, p = .058) are treated as supportive evidence, which may overstate the strength of the independent CV mirror effect.

## Abstract

*Preserved from source without summarization.*

We sought to systematically investigate how participants subjectively classify the basis of their recognition memory judgments for low and high word frequency items. We found that participants more often reported rejection processes related to the increased perceived memorability for unstudied low word frequency items (relative to high word frequency items), rather than classifying their decision on a lack of familiarity. Experiment 2 replicated this pattern and demonstrated context variability and word frequency independently influenced the subjective classifications for correct rejections. Results of Experiment 3 revealed that these differences are dependent upon having experience with both low and high frequency items. Overall, these data suggest participants’ rejection of low frequency items is more strongly related to judgments of perceived memorability, but only when they are presented in the context of high frequency items. The results are discussed in relation to distinctiveness and expected memorability.

## General Discussion

*Section: paper-level*

### Summary of Findings

The study replicated correct rejection advantages for low‑frequency words and showed that these advantages are driven by participants' perceived memorability rather than lack of familiarity or recollection, especially in mixed‑list contexts; hit‑rate advantages were not statistically evident in pure‑list designs, and subjective classification patterns varied with list composition.


### Theoretical Conclusions

- **memorability-driven rejection**
  > participants capitalize on additional processes to classify lures in a recognition memory test as new. These processes are related to general memory mechanisms that support successful recognition (i.e., familiarity and perceived memorability).

- **inherent distinctiveness account**
  > The infrequent occurrence of LWF items makes them inherently more distinctive and more memorable than HWF words. This inherent or secondary distinctiveness (Schmidt,1991) likely accounts for the increased ability to reject these stimuli.
  - Citations: Schmidt (1991)
- **contextual distinctiveness influence on judgments**
  > When both LWF and HWF items are present, the expected average memorability would not be as high, thus resulting in more Would Have Remembered judgments for LWF items. This result suggests that a Would Have Remembered judgment is not just based on the innate characteristics of the item itself, but can also be influenced by the specific test context.


### Cross-Experiment Integration

- **within- vs pure-list effects**
  > In within-subjects (mixed-list) manipulations, we found mirror effects, but we also found that correct rejections to LWF items were more likely to be attributed to a memorability process... We also replicated the correct rejection advantage for LWF items in pure lists... the pattern of subjective classifications, however, did change in the pure-list manipulation.


### Results Authors State Support Their Theory

- **memorability process advantage**
  > correct rejections to LWF items were more likely to be attributed to a memorability process and not to a lack of perceived familiarity or a recollection rejection mechanism.
  - Citations: Benjamin (2003); Brown et al. (1977); Guttentag & Carroll (1998)

### Results Authors State Challenge a Theory

- **lack of hit rate advantage in pure lists**
  > We did not find statistical evidence for the LWF hit rate advantage in pure-list manipulations, which is inconsistent with prior research.
  - Citations: Dorfman & Glanzer (1988); Gorman (1961); McCormack & Swenson (1972)

### Alternative Accounts Addressed by Authors

#### familiarity-based rejection *(named theory)*

- **Description:** Participants could reject LWF items based on an assessment that they do not have high levels of familiarity, thus not passing a criterion for having been studied.
- **How authors addressed it:** acknowledged and argued that even when afforded the Lacks Familiarity option, the rejection advantage was related to memorability rather than lack of familiarity.
- **Citations:** Arndt & Reder (2002); Guttentag & Carroll (1994); Joordens & Hockley (2000); Reder et al. (2000); Rugg et al. (1995)
#### distinctiveness heuristic *(named theory)*

- **Description:** Low-frequency items are inherently more distinctive, leading to higher memorability and easier rejection.
- **How authors addressed it:** proposed as an explanation for the increased rejection of LWF items, citing inherent distinctiveness.
- **Citations:** Schmidt (1991)
#### functional account of recollection *(named theory)*

- **Description:** Judgments of recollection depend on context and when Remember judgments are criterial for recognition decisions.
- **How authors addressed it:** drawn as an analogous explanation for rejection judgments, suggesting context may affect criterial status of Would Have Remembered responses.
- **Citations:** Bodner & Lindsay (2003); Gruppuso, Lindsay, & Kelley (1997)

## Critique

*Paper: Meeks, Knight, Brewer, Cook 2014 - Marsh Investigating the subjective reports of rejection processes in the word frequency mirror effect*

### Overall Assessment

The paper demonstrates moderate methodological and statistical shortcomings that undermine its scholarly rigor. While the experimental design addresses an interesting question, the lack of power analysis, incomplete reporting of ANOVA effects, and uncorrected multiple comparisons raise concerns about the robustness of the findings. Moreover, the theoretical framework is not tightly aligned with the evidence, as key constructs (e.g., distinctiveness) are inferred without direct measurement, and non‑significant results are not adequately integrated. These issues collectively limit the paper's evidentiary support and overall contribution to the literature.




### 2. Evidence–Theory Alignment

- **The discussion generalizes the memorability-based rejection process to a distinctiveness account without directly measuring distinctiveness, overstating theoretical support.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: While the experiments provide evidence that participants report more "Would Have Remembered" responses for low-frequency items, they do not include any direct measure of item distinctiveness. The authors therefore extend their findings to a distinctiveness explanation, which is not empirically substantiated by the reported data. This overextension may mislead readers about the strength of support for the distinctiveness account.
  - Evidence:
    - `general_discussion` — field `theoretical_conclusions`: Authors claim "inherent distinctiveness" accounts for the effect, yet no distinctiveness metric was collected.
    - `introduction` — field `theoretical_framework`: Distinctiveness heuristic is presented as a related theory but not operationalized in the experiments.

- **The authors claim that memorability-driven rejection fully explains the correct rejection advantage, yet the data show non‑significant differences in "Lacks Familiarity" and "Recall‑to‑Reject" responses, which are not integrated into the theoretical narrative.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: The theoretical claim that memorability is the primary driver of the rejection advantage is not fully supported because alternative processes were examined and found null, yet the discussion minimizes these results. Proper alignment would require addressing how these null findings constrain the memorability account.
  - Evidence:
    - `results_and_discussion`/`exp_1` — field `key_findings`: Non‑significant effects for Lacks Familiarity (p = .238) and Recall‑to‑Reject (p = .125) are reported but downplayed in the interpretation.
    - `general_discussion` — field `results_stated_to_support_theory`: Authors emphasize memorability advantage while omitting discussion of the null findings for alternative processes.




### 5. Statistical Completeness

- **The 2 (WF) × 3 (Response Type) repeated measures ANOVA in Experiment 1 does not report the main effect of Response Type, which is an expected component of the factorial analysis.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: In a factorial ANOVA, the full model includes main effects for each factor and their interaction. While the interaction is reported, the omission of the Response Type main effect leaves the statistical description incomplete. Reporting the main effect is standard practice to allow readers to assess whether the interaction is driven by overall differences across response types.
  - Evidence:
    - `methods`/`exp_1` — field `design`: Design statement specifies a 1 (word frequency) within‑subjects factor; the ANOVA reported combines WF with a 3‑level Response Type factor.
    - `results_and_discussion`/`exp_1` — field `key_findings`: Finding reports an interaction (F(2, 48) = 4.208, p = .021) and simple effects for each response type, but no statement of the main effect of Response Type.


### 6. Methodological Concerns

- **No power analysis was reported for any of the three experiments despite modest sample sizes.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: Without a priori power calculations, it is unclear whether the participant numbers (25, 34, and 69) provide sufficient sensitivity to detect the hypothesized effects. This limits confidence in null or marginal findings, especially for interaction effects that were not reported.
  - Evidence:
    - `methods`/`exp_1` — field `power_analysis`: null (no power analysis reported)
    - `methods`/`exp_2` — field `power_analysis`: null (no power analysis reported)
    - `methods`/`exp_3` — field `power_analysis`: null (no power analysis reported)

- **Counterbalancing procedures for within‑subjects manipulations are not described in any experiment.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: Proper counterbalancing is essential to control order effects in repeated‑measures designs. The absence of any description raises concerns about potential confounds influencing the observed word‑frequency and context‑variability effects.
  - Evidence:
    - `methods`/`exp_1` — field `counterbalancing`: "not reported" despite a within‑subjects word‑frequency design
    - `methods`/`exp_2` — field `counterbalancing`: "not reported" for the 2 × 2 within‑subjects design
    - `methods`/`exp_3` — field `counterbalancing`: "not reported" for the mixed design

- **Exclusion criteria are either absent or insufficiently specified across experiments.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: Transparent exclusion criteria are needed to assess potential biases from data cleaning. The limited reporting makes it impossible to evaluate whether participant removal could have systematically affected the results.
  - Evidence:
    - `methods`/`exp_1` — field `exclusions`: empty array; no criteria for data cleaning or participant removal
    - `methods`/`exp_2` — field `exclusions`: only mentions one participant excluded due to a software error; no broader criteria
    - `methods`/`exp_3` — field `exclusions`: one participant excluded for software error; no other exclusion rules

- **The study does not mention any preregistration or open‑science practices.**
  - Severity: **minor**
  - Justification: Preregistration helps guard against undisclosed analytic flexibility. Its absence reduces confidence that the reported analyses were pre‑specified rather than exploratory.
  - Evidence:
    - `methods`/`exp_1` — field `preregistration_status`: "not reported"
    - `methods`/`exp_2` — field `preregistration_status`: "not reported"
    - `methods`/`exp_3` — field `preregistration_status`: "not reported"

- **Reliability or internal consistency estimates for the subjective classification measures (e.g., "Would Have Remembered", "Lacks Familiarity") are not provided.**
  - Severity: **minor**
  - Justification: Subjective response categories are central to the theoretical claims. Without reliability evidence, it is unclear whether these measures consistently capture the intended constructs across participants.
  - Evidence:

### 7. Statistical Robustness

- **Multiple pairwise t‑tests were conducted without correction for family‑wise error rate, increasing the risk of Type I errors.**
  - Severity: **moderate**
  - Justification: The authors test many related hypotheses across response types. Without adjusting alpha, the nominal 5 % threshold is inflated, so some reported significant effects (e.g., p = .021 for the interaction, p = .015 for Remember responses) may be false positives. A correction or multivariate approach would better control error rates.
  - Evidence:
    - `results_and_discussion`/`exp_1` — field `key_findings`: Eleven independent t‑tests (e.g., hit rate, Remember, Guess, Know, correct rejection, Would Have Remembered, Lacks Familiarity, Recall to Reject, and several conditionalized measures) are reported with p‑values but no mention of Bonferroni, Holm, or other correction.

- **A marginal effect (p = .058) for context variability on correct rejection rates in Experiment 2 is described as a “marginal effect” yet is used to support the claim of independent mirror effects.**
  - Severity: **minor**
  - Justification: Treating a p‑value just above .05 as evidence can overstate the robustness of the effect. The authors should qualify conclusions more cautiously or provide confidence intervals to clarify the precision of the estimate.
  - Evidence:
    - `results_and_discussion`/`exp_2` — field `key_findings`: The report states “Main effect of WF and marginal effect of CV on correct rejection rates” with F(1,33)=3.873, p = .058.

- **Effect sizes are reported for some significant tests but omitted for non‑significant comparisons, limiting assessment of practical significance.**
  - Severity: **minor**
  - Justification: Providing effect sizes for all comparisons, including null results, would allow readers to evaluate whether non‑significant findings reflect negligible effects or simply insufficient power.
  - Evidence:
    - `results_and_discussion`/`exp_1` — field `key_findings`: Significant t‑tests include Cohen's d (e.g., d = .472, .526), whereas non‑significant tests (e.g., Know responses, Lacks Familiarity) report only p‑values without effect size metrics.
